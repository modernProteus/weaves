One thing to know before you read. I picked modest ratings on purpose and then wrote the evidence at full strength. Box one · Accomplishments & Growth ≈1,240 words

The form asks: What were your key achievements this year? Consider highlighting your contributions through the lens of Teamwork, Innovation, and Results. What capabilities have you focused on building over the past year?

This review year spanned two roles and two transitions running in opposite directions at the same time. Through January I ran AppleCare Connect as EPM. In February I moved to Solutions Architect in IS&T Customer Systems, and the months that followed meant handing off the platform I was leaving while absorbing the CS-PS solution-architecture scope.

The two roles were not separate programs. Before February I worked Panorama from the business-requirements side and built the ACC reseller-channel integration requirement set; I am a named approver on the ACC Product sections of both program BRDs. In February I moved to the architecture side of the same program with a scope well beyond ACC. Having written the requirements I then had to satisfy as the architect is a large part of why the absorption went as quickly as it did.

Teamwork

Both transitions were about making other people able to operate independently, and they overlapped. The AppleCare Connect handoff to Paul Nugent as incoming business owner ran from January into April. It was not a shutdown; it was transferring a live system to someone who had to run it. Paul needed the whole operating surface rather than the architecture alone: a focus-category framework separating analysis, operational support, testing support and strategy; the stakeholder topology across six organizations (WW Sales, Systems Support, AppleCare, Finance, Marketing and Legal) with named contacts in each; the monitoring dashboards and alerting; the trending-issue workflows together with their root-cause analyses; the data-correction and request processes; and the enhancement backlog with its supporting radar inventory. Consultation stayed open through the spring and is now rare, which is the signal that matters: the platform runs without me. A handoff is only real if the next owner can operate without the previous one, and that meant transferring the relationship map along with the architecture.

Absorbing the CS-PS scope was the inverse problem. Andrew Lambdin-Abraham was the tenured DRI and subject-matter expert for the full portfolio, and I took it on within five months of joining, not only representing every product in it but assuming primary ownership of a large program already in full swing. We set a mid-July internal target and an end-July external commitment; both were met. Andrew was on break through August while the scope ran solo, with no coverage gap across the radar queue, the cross-functional sync cadence or partner-facing coordination, including a P1 covered without escalation. Andrew got a clean exit and my manager's portfolio never had a hole in it.

The two directions were different kinds of work rather than different amounts of it. ACC was one platform owned end to end: its operations, its partner relationships, its billing integrity, its failure modes. Handing it over meant transferring an operating practice. The Panorama scope is an integration surface, spanning CDM, EventHub, DataHub, Case, GCRM Admin, Griffin, PDM, EDM and RDM among the systems involved, where the work is reasoning about how systems agree, where state should live, and which team owns which boundary. Moving between them meant changing the kind of thinking, from operating one platform deeply to holding the relationships among many. Running both directions at once through the spring meant carrying two postures simultaneously: documenting for someone else's independence while building my own.

Four reusable practices came out of the year in transferable form: a two-pass radar filing method that prevents incomplete radars reaching engineering, a stakeholder communication pattern set, the solution documentation lifecycle from brief through design through impact assessment, and a governance rule routing architectural challenges through CS-PS internal alignment before external escalation. Those practices are visible in the work itself rather than as documents on a shelf: the impact assessments and radars going to engineering are consistently structured, with content validated before filing rather than after, so engineering teams receive the same shape every time. Stakeholders can also see work status themselves rather than requesting it, since the live CS-PS portfolio dashboard suite went up this year, which directly answers feedback about designing for consumers outside my own team.

The coordination surface was wide in both halves of the year: as EPM, IS&T development and support, Finance across credit and collections, and three partner-management organizations among others, including arbitrating competing scope requests from more than seventeen business holding units; as Solutions Architect, Support Sales, Support Experience and EW Service among others. Coordinating across those time zones worked better once meeting times rotated rather than defaulting to a single region's working hours, so the early and late slots moved between groups each cycle rather than always landing on the same teams.

Results

Panorama is the umbrella, and its breadth is the point. Design ownership ran across both program tracks.

Support and Service Sales — organization lifecycle and base creation, agreement eligibility and creation, and agreement lifecycle notifications.
Support Experience — support event notifications across internal and external consumers, enterprise case management, and enterprise case and agreement lookup.

Each of those sits on a different combination of systems, and several required reconciling designs that different teams had already committed to. Both tracks were unblocked this year. The Support track had been waiting on a specification, and once it had one, engineering built from a specification rather than a discussion. On the Sales track the Agreement Lifecycle design closed, settling the ACC-boundary questions that had been open since the program started.

Three of those reconciliations are worth naming, because in each case the work was taking responsibility for a decision that had already been made and was wrong. On the Agreement Lifecycle design, three meetings across a single week had produced three incompatible positions on where organization state should live. Correlating them into one Unified Enrichment Model meant splitting the API scope so CDM orchestrates rather than stores, and separating the front-end pre-check layer from CDM's authoritative verification. That separation dissolved a perceived-duplication objection which had blocked sign-off in two previous sessions. The model survived a fourth refinement meeting with the CDM lead, the departing DRI and both managers, and was promoted from proposal to design of record. It also produced a governance commitment: no further architectural reversals on that design without CS-PS internal alignment first.

On the Premier ePoC contact-source question, three separate impact assessments from three organizations contradicted each other. Rather than arbitrate between citations, the better way through was to argue the paradigm underneath them: enterprise administrator contacts rotate and consumer contacts do not, so they cannot share a storage model even on a shared endpoint. Whoever fetches the contact must be present in the flow at send time, which resolved the design before the likely compromise position could be proposed. That went to eleven stakeholders and closed. On the Case radar scope, three committed radars were held to their existing scope ahead of a demo and additions routed to net-new radars instead, keeping prior commitments stable rather than renegotiating them under time pressure.

Alongside Panorama I carried the FY27 PLOE assessments assigned to me, which is the planning intake for next year's CS-PS work rather than delivery work, plus one project in a supporting capacity. On the V1 Support Sales playback a cut-over gap surfaced before it reached users rather than after. Through January the AppleCare Connect work was operational stewardship: holding the transactional-integrity and billing frameworks established the prior cycle at their reduced steady state, and carrying the DIA billing roadmap from approved requirements into an executing implementation program.

Innovation

The ACE Tier simplification is where architectural judgment mattered most. The business moved the deadline twice inside three weeks. Partway through it became clear our own proposed manual bulk-override approach was the wrong shape, so I brought the team to a config-collapse design instead, reframing the work as a configuration change rather than a code change, and we landed it against the moved date. Discarding our own prior proposal mid-flight was a risk worth taking; it cost some rework and it held.

Two further decisions this year were pattern choices rather than documents. For the Support track I chose a three-stage signal, classify and enrich pipeline over a single-pass handler. The detail that mattered was recognizing classification as the volatile part: the case-event taxonomy was still being renamed in workshops mid-design, and separating classification from ingestion and enrichment meant the taxonomy could churn without touching either neighbour. It did churn, repeatedly, and the pipeline held. For the Sales track I chose a correlator pattern to hold agreement state across systems that do not share a transaction boundary, which shifted ownership of whether an agreement exists to CDM and let EventHub drop persistent per-organization state. That is what let the ACC-boundary questions close rather than stay open indefinitely.

Two internal tools changed how quickly I could operate in an unfamiliar estate. Wayfinder maps the department's product infrastructure: what exists, who owns it, how it connects. Connections does the same for people and working relationships. Both were built in my first weeks in the role, and together they gave me a navigable model of Customer Systems and the wider IS&T surface. That is a substantial part of why absorbing the Panorama scope took five months rather than a year, and why I can answer ownership and dependency questions directly rather than by asking around. Both are extensions of the GenAI-assisted method I have been developing for architecture work more broadly, covering documentation lifecycle, requirements synthesis and meeting-to-decision capture, which is also what let me codify those four practices into a form other people can use. When my manager told me the Wayfinder positioning was too diffuse, the useful move was to take the critique rather than defend the framing, and the restructure that came out of it was better than what I had.

The Panorama partner-ecosystem architecture I designed in the prior cycle moved into implementation this year; this year's contribution to it was the pattern choices above.

Capabilities built

The capability I invested in most deliberately was the solution documentation lifecycle, treating solution briefs, design documents and system impact assessments as a connected progression rather than separate artifacts. Writing the brief before deep engineering engagement forces the "why this direction and not another" question early, when it is still cheap to answer. Technical depth across the Customer Systems integration surface was the second focus: CDM, PDM, IDM, EDM, SAP and Griffin, and specifically how event and agreement data moves between them. This was the growth area my manager and I identified before I took the role, and Panorama has been the vehicle for it. Third, cross-organizational governance, settling into a practice of triaging architectural challenges through CS-PS internal alignment first, which resolves most of them without external escalation and makes the ones that do escalate better formed. Finally, and most deliberately: framing work in terms of outcome rather than mechanism, naming who benefits and by how much, in their language rather than the system's. That changed how I write briefs, how I structure radars, and how I open a stakeholder conversation.

Box two · Goals & Development ≈700 words

The form asks: Knowing what our team needs to accomplish, what goals would you like to establish for the coming year? In what areas could you develop? How can your manager best support you? How will you develop skills or use emerging technologies, such as AI, to enhance your work?

The most useful thing I can say about this year is that the innovation side worked and the narration of it did not. The methods, the tooling and the design patterns are real, and their value has been mostly limited to my own work, because I have been better at building them than at documenting the results or getting them into anyone else's hands. That is the sequence I want to invert in FY27: use teamwork as the vehicle, getting the practices adopted, the tooling portable and the outcomes narrated, and let that show up on the Results side where it belongs.

Goals for FY27. Four, in priority order, each with a way to tell whether it happened. First, own a solution end to end through a full cycle, from discovery and design through delivery playback and production handoff, rather than joining mid-flight. My candidate is the Case Lifecycle Event Service: I own the design already, so carrying it into delivery and production handoff would close the loop on the SDLC understanding I have been building rather than starting from something new. Done means it is in production and handed off.

Second, get one of the four documented practices used by another SA, starting with the two-pass radar filing method. Deliberately small: one peer, one method. Done means a named person is using it without me in the room. Third, make Wayfinder usable by an incoming SA rather than only by me, scoped to the new-hire case. Done means someone joining CS-PS can answer ownership and dependency questions from it without asking me. Fourth, present architectural work directly to leadership two or three times across the year, treated as committed deliverables rather than opportunistic ones. A design playback, a program review or a written brief each count. Done is a count.

Two longer-horizon directions sit behind those, and I would not claim either as a one-year deliverable. The first is reasoning confidently about integration trade-offs, consistency models, event ordering guarantees and failure semantics without needing to consult, which accrues through repeated exposure rather than a single project. The second is moving the CS-PS scope from inherited to deliberately planned, which depends on the FY27 scope boundaries being set first.

Where I could develop, and how you can help. The hardest part of this year was not any single design; it was holding the volume without dropping anything while still learning the environment. A large inherited scope, a live program and a PLOE cycle running concurrently made the tracking burden real, and early on I was carrying too much of it in my head. That is the honest reason I built tooling for it rather than only for the design work: keeping the record of what was decided, by whom, and what was still open turned out to be the constraint on how fast I could move. It is better than it was and it is not finished.

On the leadership-visibility goal, sponsorship into the right forums would help more than anything I can arrange myself: knowing which reviews are the right venue, and having an introduction into them. This was guidance I received two years ago that I have not adequately closed, and I would rather name it than let it carry forward again. The Clarity idea assignments have also been the most effective onboarding mechanism I have had, since each one forces me into a new corner of the estate with a real deliverable attached, and I would like that to continue as a deliberate growth vehicle rather than incidental workload.

Emerging technologies and AI. I have been developing GenAI-assisted method for architecture work rather than treating AI as a drafting shortcut, and the distinction matters. The value has come from applying it to the structural parts of the job: documentation lifecycle, requirements synthesis, and turning meetings into recorded decisions with named owners instead of recollections. That is what let me codify four working practices into a form other people can pick up. It is already in use at design time, pressure-testing a solution brief before engineering sees it, which is considerably cheaper than finding the gap in review. None of it works without judgment in the loop and active context-shaping; the output is augmentation, not answers. The honest assessment of where I have spent my effort is that I have been weighted toward building the tooling rather than applying it. That is the shift for FY27: finish the tool work to the point it is portable, then spend the time on application and on making it usable by peers rather than personal. Wayfinder and Connections are the clearest test case, since they turned "who owns this and what does it connect to" from a question I had to ask into one I can answer, and that value should not stay limited to me.
